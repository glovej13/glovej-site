Adult Fan of Lego is a real scene right now, and it fits neatly into the relive-the-fun side of collecting. But the nostalgia angle is only half the story, and honestly the less interesting half. Building Lego is a focus activity. It's a mind switch, whether the box has a licensed logo on it or not. There's a set sitting unopened on a shelf right now, and the appeal isn't just what it'll look like finished. It's the hour or two of not thinking about anything except which piece comes next.

Puzzles are the smaller cousin. Not the cultural moment Lego is having, but offices have caught onto something with the 15-minute group puzzle in the break room, and I get why. For me it's the art more than the puzzle mechanic itself. I buy Star Wars and Marvel puzzles because I want that specific image in front of me, and then I look forward to actually sitting down and building it, the same way I look forward to some of the Lego still boxed up. A fair amount of both is still boxed up, if I'm honest. I buy the piece, promise myself I'll get to it, and add it to a pile that's becoming its own small collection of good intentions. That's completely on brand. Too much going on has always been the default setting.

But if I'm being honest about what I actually enjoy most in all of this, it isn't the building. It's the organizing. Sorting cards into sets. Getting a comic run complete. There's a real psychological hit in ticking off the last box on a checklist, I won't pretend there isn't, but that's not where the fun lives for me. The fun is building the list in the first place. I've been doing this since I was a kid with a database on a Commodore 64, tracking a collection that fit in a shoebox with software that took up more effort than the shoebox did. That's not a new habit. That's just always been the actual hobby, underneath the collecting.

Which is part of why building a proper site for all of it mattered to me, past just having somewhere to show it off. Almost nobody looks at the collection database. Doesn't matter. I still want a full-featured site I can dig through and filter myself, the same way I wanted that database on the Commodore 64 that nobody else ever saw either. The audience was never really the point.

One more piece of this worth naming plainly: most of the sorting and organizing happens with the TV on. Not because the show is background noise I'm ignoring. Because the show usually isn't quite enough on its own. A procedural I've half-seen before, something with plenty of dialogue but not much that demands full attention, leaves a gap. Sorting a stack of cards, working a corner of a puzzle, fills exactly that gap. Neither one gets me to full focus alone. Together they add up to it.

That's the other side of turning off. Not shutting the brain down completely the way three books in six days does. Filling in the part a show didn't need, with something that has its own quiet, repetitive pull. Different tool, same job.

— Josh Glover is a collector based in Ottawa. He collects Star Wars, Star Trek, Doctor Who, hockey cards, and a few other things he can't fully explain.
