My wife and I have a long history of watching home makeover shows together. Trading Spaces first. Then the TLC shows where someone's closet gets torn apart and rebuilt in a weekend. We used to joke about it — how fun it'd be to get picked, to have some stranger with a truck full of paint show up and fix a room we'd been ignoring for years. It was never a serious wish. Just the kind of thing you say during a commercial break.

Then Queer Eye came along, and something about it sat differently.

I still liked the makeover part. The wardrobe, the space, all of it — that side of the show genuinely worked for me the way it always had. But that wasn't what I found myself wanting. What I wanted was to talk to Karamo. Not to be on the show, exactly. To sit across from him and talk.

I didn't know what I would have said. I'm not sure I knew what I was even feeling, only that it was pointed at him specifically, not at the show, not at the format, not at the idea of being picked. Just him. Out of five people whose job was to fix something about your life, I wanted the one whose job was to fix nothing you could see.

I think there were two things going on. One is something a lot of guys deal with. You can have friends who'd say they're there for you, and mean it, but it's still harder to open up to someone you'll see at the rink next week than to someone who has no other stake in your life. Distance makes it easier to say the thing out loud. The other is that Karamo was just good at it. He had a way of getting people talking about things they didn't want to, or didn't even know were sitting there. Some of that's a trained skill, close to what a therapist does. But it also came from him. Someone who could listen without judging, and knew where to hone in. Some of that's his background. Some of it's just who he is.

I've thought about that more since than I did at the time. It wasn't a decision to do anything. It wasn't even really a thought, most days. More like a low pull I didn't examine too closely, the way you notice a sound in another room and decide it's nothing without actually checking. Looking back, it's the earliest point I can find where some part of me was already circling something about my own mental health, long before I would have used that phrase for it, or admitted there was anything to circle.

I read Karamo's memoir last year. The actual book, not the show version of him. I wrote about it in the reading section here, the short version: a Jamaican-Cuban kid who became the first openly gay Black man on The Real World, who built a career and a public identity around exactly the kind of conversation I'd wanted to have with him, years before I read a word he'd written. I don't know if that's coincidence or if I was pulled toward the show because that's what he does on it. Either way, it tracks.

I don't talk about this much. Typing it here is easier than saying it out loud has ever been.

Here's the part I keep coming back to, though. That pull sat quiet for years. It didn't get louder, it didn't get acted on, it just stayed there, unremarked, while I kept doing everything else a normal week requires. That's the part I'd actually want someone else to hear, if anyone reading this recognizes the shape of it: the wanting doesn't have to be dramatic to be real. It doesn't have to announce itself as a mental health thing to be one. Mine didn't. It looked like wanting to talk to a guy on a reality show, and I let it sit there for years because it didn't look like anything worth acting on.

I'm not going to tell you what to do with that if it's true for you too. I didn't do anything with it for a long time either. But if there's a version of this for you — a specific person you keep wanting to talk to, a conversation you keep half-having in your head and never starting. I don't think that's nothing. I think it's worth paying attention to, even long before you're ready to do anything about it.

I still don't fully know what I would have said to Karamo. I know a little more now about why I wanted to.
