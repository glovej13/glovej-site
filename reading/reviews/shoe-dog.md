*The founder story behind Nike, told by someone who spends most of the book sounding less like a business legend and more like a guy who wasn't sure it was going to work.*

This is not a polished business book, and that's the whole reason it works. Knight wrote a paper at Stanford asking whether Japanese running shoes could do to German running shoes what Japanese cameras did to German cameras. His professor gave him an A. Then he sat on the idea for years, kept his accounting job, and built the company that became Nike on the side until the side project ran out of room to stay a side project.

I read this about two years ago, before a lot of what I'm dealing with now. At the time, what stuck with me was the throughline about not giving up, failure as a step on the way to something, not a verdict on whether you should keep going. Banks pulling credit without warning, suppliers threatening to walk, years where the company should have died and didn't. Knight's answer to every crisis was to keep moving instead of retreating, and that was the lesson I took at face value.

If I'm honest, it also had me looking sideways at a couple of my own business ideas I'd been sitting on. Nothing came of that at the time, and I think I already knew why. My safety net was too solid to give up on. It's a lot easier to go all in when you're already walking a fine line toward having nothing left to lose. Giving up a well-paying job for a bet like that just wasn't in my risk appetite, even if it was very much alive in the adventure-dreaming part of my brain.

"The cowards never started and the weak died along the way." That line has stuck around in business circles for a reason, and reading it in the middle of one of the company's near-death stretches, it lands harder than it does as a quote on its own.

Rereading my own notes on it now, with a job search underway and RTF Consulting actually being built instead of just sat on, that line reads differently than it did two years ago. Not as a verdict on the decision I made then. More as a nudge to actually weigh the risk this time, instead of just admiring it from a safe distance.

Worth the read either way. It's earned its spot as a business classic, but go in expecting a memoir, not a playbook.
