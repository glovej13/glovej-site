*Picked this up expecting hockey stories. Got a leadership book instead, which is exactly the trap I wanted to fall into.*

I'm an Oilers fan, so this one was never a hard sell. Six Cups, the Gretzky-era dynasty, the Rangers captaincy in '94. What I didn't expect was how little of the book is actually about hockey. It's about how you get a group of talented, ego-driven people to actually play for each other, and Messier keeps circling back to the same idea from a dozen different angles.

The distinction that stuck with me most: motivating people isn't the same as inspiring them. Motivation needs constant reinforcement, carrots and sticks, and it wears people down or makes them resentful over time. Inspiration is different. Give people a shared purpose and they find their own reasons to show up. That's the whole job, and it's a lot harder than it sounds because you can't fake it.

I've coached both my sons' hockey teams and sat on the other side of the table as a league exec, and I've always believed that carries over into work, not the other way around. The problems have nothing in common on the surface, but the practicalities of running a team, communicating expectations, handling someone who isn't pulling their weight, keeping a group bought into something bigger than themselves, land close enough between a locker room and a boardroom that this book's whole theme was never a hard sell for me. I'd already watched most of it be true.

The specific parts that landed hardest were about clarity. The players who needed to be told exactly what was expected of them and where they stood, every time, no ambiguity. The ones who fell apart the moment leadership got theatrical instead of plain. Messier's dad ran his team that way, and it's the same thing I try to do at work, minus the skates.

"A leader's job is actually very simple: Make those around you better." That's page 275, near the end, and it's the one line I kept coming back to after I put the book down. Not because it's a new idea. Because it's the right size for how big the job actually is. If I'm honest about how I measure my own success as a leader, it's always come down to watching people who worked for me go further in their careers than where they started with me. That's not really different from a coach measuring success by a player moving up to a higher level. Same reward, different rink.

The parts about accountability without cruelty landed harder than I expected. Cutting a player who won't do the dirty work isn't about punishment, it's about clarity, and there's a version of that in every team I've ever run. No theatrics needed. Just a clean, consistent line about what's required.

Worth reading whether or not you follow hockey. Just go in expecting a leadership book with a hockey memoir wrapped around it, not the other way around.
