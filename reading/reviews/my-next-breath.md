*A memoir about a snowplow that should have killed him, and the very small unit of measurement he used to survive what came after.*

I'll admit the honest reason I picked this up: Renner plays Hawkeye, my favorite Avenger. Turns out the real story is the more heroic one, and it doesn't need a bow.

I knew the outline going in. Renner nearly died protecting his nephew from a runaway snowplow on New Year's Day 2023. What I didn't know was how unflinching the book is about the actual physical toll. The surgeries, the setbacks, the months where recovery meant relearning things most of us never think about once. It's not written for comfort, and it's better for it.

The idea that stuck with me is a small one: the only thing anyone actually controls is their own perception of what's happening to them. Renner didn't come out of a coma feeling positive. He came out of it and made a decision to be, one day at a time, and kept remaking that decision every day after. That's a different thing than optimism, and the book is honest about the difference.

I read this in January 2025, which is its own kind of timing. Turning 50 was close enough to feel real, and I tried to borrow some of Renner's stubbornness to finally eat better and move more before I got there. If he could push through what he went through, surely I could manage a diet and a gym habit. It didn't stick, not really, no matter how much I wanted his determination to rub off on me. The honest bottom line is that willing yourself into motivation from someone else's story is a lot harder than it sounds, even when the story is a genuinely good one.

His mantra through recovery was "Milestones over tombstones." Small wins outweighing the alternative, deliberately, on purpose, every single day. It's a simple phrase and I don't think it's supposed to be more than that. Some of the best ideas in this book aren't complicated. They're just repeated until they hold.

There's a section on pain as a kind of overprotective language your body speaks to you, and another on how the noise we all carry around in our pockets makes it harder to sit with anything real. Neither of those needed the accident to be true. He just had more reason than most of us to actually notice them.

Last fall I ended up with my own much smaller version of a forced stop. A ruptured Achilles and a posterior shoulder dislocation, three days in the hospital, one leg and one arm out on opposite sides of my body. It's more disabling than it sounds until you've actually tried to get around that way. I thought about this book more than once during that stretch, and I genuinely marveled at how Renner pushed through something so much bigger than what I was dealing with. I found my own version hard enough. It helped a little, having that comparison in my head. Not as much as I wanted it to.

If I'm honest, I think I'm closer to that kind of battle now than I was reading it the first time, not the physical version, but the job search and getting older and the general state of things version. I still try to pull whatever I can from this one when I need it.

This one's a harder read than the others on this list. Worth it, but don't go in looking for a light afternoon.
